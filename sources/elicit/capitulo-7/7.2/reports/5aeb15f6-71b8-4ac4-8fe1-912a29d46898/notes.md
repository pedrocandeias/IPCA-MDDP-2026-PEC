# Digital Prosthetic Customization: A Dual Perspective Analysis

- `report_id`: `5aeb15f6-71b8-4ac4-8fe1-912a29d46898`
- URL: https://elicit.com/review/5aeb15f6-71b8-4ac4-8fe1-912a29d46898
- status: `completed`

## Summary

Digital prosthetic customisation platforms operate as asymmetric collaboration spaces that concentrate configurative authority and decision-making responsibility with technical professionals in socket design contexts while granting end users meaningful but supervised agency in control customisation contexts, with automation functioning throughout as a cognitive offload mechanism that encodes clinical expertise rather than replacing it—and the field's central gap is the near-absence of direct evidence for upper-limb socket design platforms, leaving the transferability of well-developed lower-limb paradigms unvalidated.

## Abstract

Digital prosthetic customisation platforms consistently function as asymmetric collaboration spaces rather than neutral interfaces, structuring differentiated experiences for technical professionals and end users in ways that reflect and reinforce existing clinical hierarchies. In socket design systems—where the evidence base is dominated by lower-limb transferable work—prosthetists and orthopaedic technicians are the primary configurative agents, with platforms encoding their tacit knowledge as automated rules and virtual tools[Stella Gabbiadini, 2011; G. Colombo et al., 2018], while patients serve primarily as data sources and comfort evaluators rather than design participants[G. Colombo et al., 2010; F. Mbithi et al., 2024]. Parametric automation, FEA-based simulation, and evidence-generated design approaches can match clinician-led designs on aggregate comfort outcomes—the Mbithi et al. crossover trials found no statistically significant difference between evidence-generated and CAD/CAM sockets (median SCS 8.6 vs. 8.8, p = 0.43)[F. Mbithi et al., 2025]—but qualitative findings consistently show that clinical expertise retains irreplaceable relational, communicative, and contextually adaptive functions that algorithms do not reproduce[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; A. Eshraghi et al., 2024]. In control customisation platforms, by contrast, end users hold meaningful configurative authority: systems for user-preferred tuning of robotic knees[Abbas Alili et al., 2023], semi-autonomous gaze-EMG interfaces[Rebecca J. Greene et al., 2024], and multimodal shared control[Chunhao Peng et al., 2025] all demonstrate that user preference over control behaviour is something only the user can determine, and that automation functions most effectively as a cognitive offload mechanism that amplifies rather than displaces user agency. Intelligibility of platform logic emerges as a distinct dimension of user experience: making classifier decision boundaries directly visible to users improved myoelectric control proficiency[Ruichen Yang et al., 2025], and training strategies oriented toward class separability produced more accurate user mental models of system behaviour[Vaynee Sungeelee et al., 2024], while clinicians performing digital socket rectification reported reduced confidence due to the loss of tactile feedback that traditional casting provides[A. Eshraghi et al., 2024]. Supervision structures are well developed in socket design contexts through FEA pressure visualisation and tiered parameter access[R. Morotti, 2014; Xiaolu Bai et al., 2024], but less formalised in control systems. The most consequential gap in the literature concerns upper-limb socket design specifically: despite substantial evidence from lower-limb digital fabrication platforms, directly analogous systems for trans-radial and trans-humeral sockets remain underdeveloped and undertested[Jennifer Olsen et al., 2020; B. Oldfrey et al., 2024], and whether lower-limb design paradigms, parametric tools, and evidence-generation methods transfer to upper-limb biomechanical and functional requirements is an empirical question the current corpus cannot answer.

The corpus divides broadly into three platform categories: physical design and socket fabrication systems (predominantly lower-limb, with growing upper-limb representation), control customisation interfaces (predominantly upper-limb), and training and visualisation environments (primarily upper-limb). Of the 80 sources, approximately 42 provide direct evidence from upper-limb prosthetic contexts, while the remaining 38 offer transferable evidence from lower-limb prosthetics, orthotics, rehabilitation devices, or assistive technology. Full texts were retrieved for 24 sources; the remaining 56 were available only as abstracts, which constrains the depth of extraction for those entries and limits conclusions about internal study details.

## Differentiated User Roles and Structured Experiences

### The Asymmetric Architecture of Professional vs. End-User Access

Across the corpus, the most consistent structural feature of digital prosthetic customisation platforms is their asymmetric organisation of user roles. Rather than providing a single interface to all participants, platforms consistently partition access, capability, and responsibility between technical professionals and end users. This asymmetry is not incidental but constitutes the foundational logic of how these systems are designed.

On the technical professional side, the depth and type of configuration access varies by discipline. Prosthetists and orthopaedic technicians are the primary operators of socket design platforms. In the Socket Modelling Assistant (SMA/SMA²) series developed at the University of Bergamo, the tool is explicitly oriented around the workflow and tacit knowledge of the orthopaedic technician, encoding their traditional manipulations as virtual operations[Stella Gabbiadini, 2011; G. Colombo et al., 2018]. SMA² provides interactive tools including automatic rectification, mesh editing, and template-based trim-line generation, all structured to reflect how technicians work rather than how patients experience the device[G. Colombo et al., 2018]. The platform is explicitly described as making design "more reliable and repeatable compared to the conventional process, but still enough similar to it to be accepted by the involved personnel"[G. Colombo et al., 2018], underscoring that professional acceptance, not patient-centricity, anchors the design rationale. Similarly, the knowledge-based framework described by Gabbiadini integrates expert knowledge via virtual assistants that guide technicians step by step through selection and sizing decisions, with automated execution "when possible"[Stella Gabbiadini, 2011]. In this architecture, the platform amplifies professional expertise rather than distributing it.

Clinicians occupy a distinct but also privileged role in control customisation contexts. The early MyoWizard and MyoAssistant tools described by Wells et al. provided prosthetists and therapists with the ability to select pre-defined control strategies, program them into micro-controllers, and fine-tune gain and threshold parameters through a graphical signal display[D. Wells et al., 2002; D. Wells et al., 2002]. This clinician-centric model persists through the 2024 Clinical Tuning Interface (CTI) for powered knee-ankle prostheses, which translates clinical tuning concepts directly into model adjustments, enabling a prosthetist to complete full multi-activity configuration in under 20 minutes compared with hours of engineer-led parameter adjustment[Emma Reznick et al., 2024; Emma Reznick et al., 2024]. The CTI concentrates final decision-making authority in the prosthetist[Emma Reznick et al., 2024]. Quintero et al.'s clinician control interface for a powered knee-ankle prosthesis likewise explicitly repositions the prosthetist as the operator of a virtual constraint scheme, replacing the researcher or engineer as the primary tuner[David Quintero et al., 2018; David Quintero et al., 2018]. Both systems function as structured collaboration spaces in which clinical authority is formalised through interface design.

End users, by contrast, are most commonly positioned as subjects of measurement, providers of subjective feedback, or beneficiaries of decisions made upstream. In the majority of socket design platforms—including the Colombo et al. paradigm series[G. Colombo et al., 2010; G. Colombo et al., 2010a; G. Colombo et al., 2011], the Morotti virtual testing lab[R. Morotti, 2014], and the Bonacini and Buldo digital methodology[D. Bonacini & Gerardina Buldo, 2024]—patients provide data (residual limb scans, gait recordings, pressure measurements) and receive devices, but are not described as operating design interfaces or making configuration selections. The patient's role as a data source rather than a design agent is a consistent structural characteristic of socket design platforms, regardless of whether the domain is lower- or upper-limb. Even in studies where patient comfort scores are the primary outcome measure, such as the Mbithi et al. crossover trials of evidence-generated sockets[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024], the patient's function is evaluative rather than configurative: they wear both socket types and report comfort, but the design decisions are made by prosthetists using data from previous clinical records.

There are meaningful exceptions and gradations. In prosthesis control platforms, end users are considerably more active as co-configurers. The robotic knee tuning framework of Alili et al. provides a User-Controlled Interface that allows users to select their preferred knee kinematic profiles by modifying control points in a four-dimensional space[Abbas Alili et al., 2023; Abbas Alili et al., 2023], with a reinforcement learning algorithm then adjusting the 12 underlying impedance parameters to match those preferences. Users in that study could accurately and consistently identify their preferred control profiles in blinded comparative testing[Abbas Alili et al., 2023], suggesting genuine end-user participation in functional configuration rather than merely aesthetic or comfort feedback. The Chunhao Peng et al. multimodal shared control system similarly frames users as having "full control authority" via EMG and voice, with system autonomy acting as a facilitator rather than a replacement of user intent[Chunhao Peng et al., 2025]. The smartphone-based control platform of Hepp et al. lets patients preselect and switch grip patterns and choose from three control algorithms, placing meaningful functional customisation in the hands of the user[D. Hepp et al., 2015; D. Hepp et al., 2015].

The Peixoto et al. online personalisation platform for myoelectric prostheses represents the furthest shift toward end-user autonomy in the corpus: it is explicitly designed to allow people with upper limb amputation to personalise aesthetics independently, with digital accessibility features, a support chatbot, and confirmatory interaction mechanisms[Sara Peixoto et al., 2025; Sara Peixoto et al., 2025; Sara Peixoto et al., 2025]. System Usability Scale scores of 79.5 to 92.5 across three participant groups[Sara Peixoto et al., 2025] indicate that the inclusive design approach succeeded in making the platform genuinely accessible to end users without professional mediation. However, this platform focuses on aesthetic rather than functional configuration, which limits its comparability with socket design or control tuning systems.

### Role Differentiation by Platform Type

The pattern across the corpus is that the degree of end-user participation in configuration is strongly determined by platform type. In socket fabrication and physical design systems, end users have little configurative agency; in control customisation interfaces, they have moderate to high agency; in training and visualisation platforms, they are the primary operators, though under technical professional supervision.

This pattern is illustrated by contrasting the Regazzoni et al. virtual platform for lower-limb prosthesis design[D. Regazzoni et al., 2019]—in which prosthetists and technicians are the primary users and the application is noted as useful for "training purpose for junior orthopedic technicians"[D. Regazzoni et al., 2019]—with the VR training system of Perry et al.[Briana N. Perry et al., 2018], in which individuals with upper extremity loss are the primary operators of a stereoscopic, motion-controlled avatar environment, following and controlling virtual limb representations as a training intervention. In the latter, technical professionals develop and administer the platform but are not its operational users[Briana N. Perry et al., 2018]. The Rozevink et al. study of a VR prosthesis control training environment similarly found that both therapists and prosthesis users operated the system, but with meaningfully different profiles: therapists scored significantly higher on subscales of conscious processing and visual attention[S. Rozevink et al., 2025], reflecting their professional surveillance function, while prosthesis users experienced lower perceived competence despite similar enjoyment and usability scores.

Occupational therapists constitute a third distinct user category in the corpus, sitting between prosthetists and patients in terms of configurative authority. The parametric design tool evaluated by Li and Aflatoony allows OTs to modify existing 3D handgrip models by entering individual hand measurements and selecting from configurable parameters including dimensions, weight, and material[Mixuan Li & L. Aflatoony, 2025; Mixuan Li & L. Aflatoony, 2025], with the platform designed so that OTs—not the patients they serve, and not engineers—are the primary operators[Mixuan Li & L. Aflatoony, 2025]. This positions OTs as first-line customisers in the assistive technology context, a configuration that could be transferable to prosthetics settings. Similarly, InHwa Lee et al. identify occupational therapists as the source of user needs specifications for the MyoTrainXR system, positioning them as a form of surrogate end-user representative in the design process[InHwa Lee et al., 2026].

Multi-stakeholder platforms that attempt to involve diverse roles simultaneously are described in several sources but tend to resolve toward asymmetric hierarchies when actual configuration decisions must be made. The participatory design protocol of Chu et al. involves an occupational therapist, an engineer, an experienced child user, and a parent in a three-corner collaborative design process[Ai Reen Chu et al., 2024; Ai Reen Chu et al., 2024], but specific mechanisms for mediating between these roles when preferences conflict are not elaborated. The Whatley et al. and Zhou et al. generative co-design systems for aesthetic prosthetics represent a more genuinely symmetric collaboration between algorithm and human user, in which disabled dance artists generate aesthetic seeds through movement that are directly applied to prosthetic covers[Sarah Whatley et al., 2023; Feng Zhou et al., 2023]; here, the technical professional is largely absent from the operational loop, and the platform functions as a relatively neutral aesthetic translation engine. However, these systems address only cosmetic customisation and have not been evaluated in functional prosthetic fitting contexts.

## Platform Mechanisms for Design Mediation

### Parametric Configuration and Design Automation

Parametric configuration—the use of structured, adjustable parameters to drive design variation—is present across nearly all platform types in the corpus, though with very different levels of user exposure and interpretability. In socket design systems, parametric logic operates largely beneath the user interface, encoding clinical knowledge as automated rules. The SMA² system uses a library of templates and embedded rules drawn from orthopaedic technician practice, generating trim-line positions and rectification operations automatically based on patient measurements[G. Colombo et al., 2018]. The evidence-generated socket systems of Mbithi et al. extend this logic further, using a statistical shape model and Bayesian inference over 163 previous transtibial CASD records to generate an individualised starting-point socket without clinician input[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024]. The clinical trial found no statistically significant difference in socket comfort score between evidence-generated sockets (median 8.6) and clinician-led CAD/CAM controls (median 8.8), with p = 0.43 and effect size = 0.05[F. Mbithi et al., 2025], suggesting that automated parametric generation can match clinical practice at the aggregate level while reducing baseline design effort.

The PACT algorithm for transradial socket design represents the most fully automated parametric design system in the corpus for upper-limb contexts[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026]. PACT retrieves the most similar limb-socket pair from a reference library and applies isotropic and anisotropic scaling to match the input limb geometry, delivering a first-draft socket in approximately 13 seconds with surface discrepancies of 2.11 ± 0.51 mm and volume differences of 2.74 ± 2.56% relative to prosthetist-designed references[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026]. Slice-wise area differences were within ±10% for most of the socket length, with larger errors near the proximal trimline and distal tip[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026]. The system systematically undersizes sockets for paediatric clients and oversizes them for adults, an age-related bias that the authors identify as a target for rule-based or measurement-informed refinement[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026]. This finding illustrates both the power and the limitation of purely data-driven parametric design: the algorithm learns from the distribution of past clinical decisions, including any systematic biases in that distribution.

In control customisation contexts, parametric configuration is more directly visible to both clinicians and users. The CTI of Reznick et al. provides prosthetists with clinically interpretable tuning parameters that are translated internally into model adjustments across multiple walking tasks and sit-stand transitions[Emma Reznick et al., 2024; Emma Reznick et al., 2024], enabling full multi-activity personalisation with an average iteration cycle of approximately 2 minutes for walking and 1 minute for sit-stand[Emma Reznick et al., 2024]. The Alili et al. user-controlled interface uses a graphical representation of a four-dimensional kinematic preference space, allowing users to modify control points that are then mapped to 12 impedance parameters via reinforcement learning[Abbas Alili et al., 2023; Abbas Alili et al., 2023]. Average tuning time in that system was 2.5 minutes[Abbas Alili et al., 2023]. These clinician- and user-facing parametric interfaces represent a middle ground between fully automated design and the traditional trial-and-error expert adjustment described in earlier work such as Wells et al.[D. Wells et al., 2002].

The Colombo et al. series consistently used low-cost hand-tracking devices—specifically the Leap Motion controller—to make parametric socket manipulation more natural and accessible to prosthetists. Preliminary tests with ten volunteers showed that beginners required repeated gesture attempts while experts performed tasks with fewer trials[G. Colombo et al., 2015], with the icon-based feedback system found more useful by novices than experts[G. Colombo et al., 2015]. The progression from SMA in 2010[G. Colombo et al., 2010a] through SMA² in 2018[G. Colombo et al., 2018] reflects an iterative design trajectory in which automation, NUI, and embedded knowledge management were progressively integrated into a unified parametric design environment.

### Visualisation and Simulation as Mediation Tools

Visualisation capabilities serve multiple functions in the corpus: as tools for quality assurance, as communication bridges between professionals and patients, and as aids for building intelligibility of platform-generated outputs. The most technically sophisticated visualisation infrastructure in the socket design domain involves finite element analysis (FEA) coupled with 3D models of the residual limb. In the Morotti virtual testing laboratory, FEA is integrated directly into SMA through automated pipelines that generate pressure distribution colour maps, highlight areas requiring modification, and re-run simulation after each design iteration[R. Morotti, 2014; R. Morotti, 2014]. The Lee et al. digital design framework for transtibial interfaces similarly uses FEA to simulate limb deformation under load and to design custom liners and sockets, with results supporting statistically significant reductions in pain during walking (p \< 0.05) and significant decreases in anterior distal tibial pressure (p \< 0.05) compared with conventional methods[Duncan R. C. Lee et al., 2024].

In control and training systems, visualisation functions primarily as a feedback mechanism to close the loop between user action and system state. The Reviewer interface developed by Yang et al. projects EMG signals into a three-dimensional decision space, displaying coloured clusters for each gesture class and a real-time cursor indicating the user's current muscle activity relative to classification boundaries[Ruichen Yang et al., 2025]. In a ten-session longitudinal study with 12 able-bodied participants, the Reviewer group showed significantly higher Fitts' Law task completion rates, reduced overshoot, and improved path efficiency compared with a conventional virtual arm visualisation group[Ruichen Yang et al., 2025]. The mechanism is meaningful: by making the classifier's internal logic directly visible to the user, the Reviewer supports a form of mutual adaptation in which users can deliberately reshape their EMG patterns to improve separability, rather than relying on trial-and-error adjustment. The Sungeelee et al. study on teaching strategies provides complementary evidence, finding that guiding users to train on the least separable gesture class (learner-led strategy) produced larger accuracy gains and more accurate mental models of system behaviour than either random cuing or user-selected training[Vaynee Sungeelee et al., 2024]; participants who focused on class separability developed better predictive understanding of prosthesis recognition failures[Vaynee Sungeelee et al., 2024].

VR and AR environments introduce a qualitatively different form of visualisation, one that immerses users in a simulated operational context rather than displaying abstract signals. The Górski et al. evaluation of XR technologies in prosthetic development found that Mixed Reality was ranked highest by an expert panel for ease of use, comfort, and fluency, while VR excelled in content quality and realism, and AR received comparatively lower scores despite positive evaluations overall[F. Górski et al., 2023; F. Górski et al., 2023]. Notably, older participants with less digital experience reported more difficulties with user interface elements and greater susceptibility to cyber sickness[F. Górski et al., 2023], indicating that visualisation modality interacts with user characteristics in ways that affect platform accessibility. The MyoTrainXR commercial VR intervention for myoelectric training achieved regulatory registration and demonstrated increased patient motivation and confidence in clinical pilots, but also surfaced setup complexity and sensor ergonomics as implementation barriers[Ricardo Garcia-Rosas et al., 2026]. Liu et al. found in a 2026 comparison study that pattern recognition control showed consistent performance between physical device and VR settings, while direct control and continuous control produced lower performance in VR, likely due to reduced haptic feedback and stricter spatial-alignment requirements[Yunmei Liu et al., 2026]; this finding has direct implications for how much confidence can be placed in VR-based evaluation of control modes.

The Vitali 2016 Mixed Reality environment VOLAB, which combined Kinect v2 body tracking, Oculus Rift head-mounted display, and Leap Motion hand tracking, represents an early attempt to create an immersive socket design workspace[A. Vitali, 2016]. Preliminary tests confirmed real-time performance and hand interaction capabilities, but residual limb reconstruction quality was identified as a limitation[A. Vitali, 2016]. AR applications for prosthetic fitting have also been explored in the Fahd Ahmed et al. system, which uses MediaPipe-based pose estimation and AR overlay to enable real-time visualisation of customised prosthetics on the patient's body, with pose detection accuracy within ±1.2 cm of manual measurements and stable rendering at 28+ FPS[Fahd Ahmed et al., 2025]. The Vlachostergiou Aquaborg project used an AR app to map speculative prosthetic designs onto the human body for user evaluation, bridging generative design and embodied previewing in a co-creative context[Maria Vlachostergiou, 2022].

### Human-in-the-Loop Assistance and Iterative Adjustment

Human-in-the-loop (HITL) assistance mechanisms vary widely across the corpus in terms of which human is in the loop and at what stage. In socket design platforms, the human in the loop is almost exclusively the technical professional. SMA² structures human involvement through template-based initialisation followed by clinician-guided modification using virtual tools that emulate traditional hand operations[G. Colombo et al., 2018]; the clinician intervenes at defined decision points while automated processes handle geometric reconstruction and preliminary fitting. The evidence-generated socket systems of Mbithi et al. position human intervention later in the process: the algorithm generates a complete first-pass socket, after which the prosthetist applies the brim line and makes modifications at the fitting session based on patient feedback[F. Mbithi et al., 2024; F. Mbithi et al., 2024]. This architecture explicitly reserves the prosthetist's time for high-skill customisation and patient communication, with the platform handling baseline design[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024].

Iterative adjustment in socket design typically involves cycles of simulated fitting, modification, and re-simulation. In the Morotti virtual testing lab, the system highlights pressure areas exceeding maximum thresholds, the prosthetist or the system automatically adjusts geometry, and the FEA is re-executed[R. Morotti, 2014]; multiple such cycles optimise the socket shape before any physical fabrication occurs. The Bonacini and Buldo digital methodology similarly embeds a deformation-based compression step driven by FEM simulation into the design-before-manufacture workflow, with validation via gait analysis[D. Bonacini & Gerardina Buldo, 2024; D. Bonacini & Gerardina Buldo, 2024]. In these iterative models, decision authority is shared between automated optimisation and human oversight, but the interface does not make this sharing explicitly visible to the user; the prosthetist reviews outputs rather than monitoring or adjusting the optimisation logic itself.

Iterative adjustment in control customisation systems is more visible and interactive. The CTI of Reznick et al. supports a two-minute observe-adjust-reprocess cycle, with prosthetists directly modifying kinematic and impedance parameters and immediately observing the resulting behaviour change[Emma Reznick et al., 2024]. The Quintero et al. CCI uses a virtual constraint scheme in which the clinician designs joint kinematic trajectories that directly drive the prosthesis controller, with the trajectory design itself constituting the primary configuration step[David Quintero et al., 2018]; this approach enabled a prosthetist to configure a powered knee-ankle prosthesis for a transfemoral user in under ten minutes, compared with hours of engineer-led parameter search[David Quintero et al., 2018]. The Alili et al. user-controlled interface similarly supports rapid iteration, with users selecting kinematic profiles, observing prosthesis behaviour, and revising preferences within a single session[Abbas Alili et al., 2023].

In training systems, iterative adjustment takes the form of progressive learning with feedback. The Perry et al. VR training platform showed significant improvement in active motor scores over time for Basic (β = 0.125, p = 0.022) and Advanced (β = 0.45, p = 0.001) motion sets, with 95% classification accuracy achieved for Basic sets by all participants and for Advanced and Digit sets by 50% of participants[Briana N. Perry et al., 2018]. The InHwa Lee et al. MyoTrainXR system showed improvement from 86.1 ± 5.8% to 98.5 ± 3.7% success rate for intact-limb participants and from 0–40% to 100% for upper-limb-loss participants across four 45-minute training sessions[InHwa Lee et al., 2026], with median iteration completion time decreasing from 19.4 to 15.8 seconds (p \< 0.05)[InHwa Lee et al., 2026]. The closed-loop data acquisition strategy of Brusamento et al. improved myocontrol performance by guiding users through acoustic feedback to provide training data in underrepresented regions of the input space[Donato Brusamento et al., 2020; Donato Brusamento et al., 2020], outperforming standard open-loop acquisition in a study involving one prosthetic hand and activities inspired by daily living[Donato Brusamento et al., 2020].

Variant comparison—the ability to evaluate multiple design options simultaneously or sequentially—is less systematically supported. In socket design, the Gabbiadini framework permits rapid generation of alternative configurations[Stella Gabbiadini, 2011], and the SMA² system supports multi-material 3D printing as a means of realising design variants[G. Colombo et al., 2018], but structured side-by-side comparison tools are not described. In control systems, the Alili et al. blinded comparative study showed that users could reliably identify their preferred control profile when presented with alternatives[Abbas Alili et al., 2023], and Vaynee Sungeelee et al. used a post-training questionnaire to assess participants' predictive understanding of the system's recognition behaviour[Vaynee Sungeelee et al., 2024]. Yunmei Liu et al. explicitly evaluated three control modes (direct control, pattern recognition, continuous control) in parallel across VR and physical device settings[Yunmei Liu et al., 2026], providing one of the more systematic variant comparisons in the corpus; their finding that pattern recognition was uniquely robust to VR's sensory limitations while direct control showed lower cognitive workload in VR[Yunmei Liu et al., 2026] illustrates how structured variant comparison can yield non-obvious design insights.

### Supervision and Decision-Making Responsibility

Supervision mechanisms—tools that enable oversight of design decisions by a responsible professional—are most developed in the lower-limb socket design domain, where clinical accountability is clearest. In the SMA² system, experienced technicians can use the platform to mentor junior designers, with the platform's embedded rules providing a form of algorithmic supervision of novice decisions[G. Colombo et al., 2018; G. Colombo et al., 2018]. The Colombo et al. 2011 framework validation involved the technical staff of a certified orthopaedic laboratory reviewing platform-generated configurations against their own expertise[G. Colombo et al., 2011]. The FEA-based systems of Morotti[R. Morotti, 2014] and Bonacini and Buldo[D. Bonacini & Gerardina Buldo, 2024] use pressure distribution visualisation as a supervisory feedback mechanism, allowing the prosthetist to identify and correct suboptimal designs before fabrication.

In control customisation systems, supervision is less formalised but still present. The Wells et al. MyoWizard and MyoAssistant system provides graphical display of signals during parameter adjustment, enabling prosthetists to observe the effect of changes before committing them to the device[D. Wells et al., 2002]. The Xiaolu Bai et al. framework for intelligent lower-limb prosthesis interaction distinguishes two classes of user-system interaction—direct interaction during locomotion and indirect interaction through a UCI to address current or foreseeable challenges—and notes that certain parameters are accessible only to prosthetists, creating a tiered supervision structure in which clinical oversight of high-stakes settings is preserved[Xiaolu Bai et al., 2024; Xiaolu Bai et al., 2024]. The framework explicitly identifies transparency—the degree to which wearers can understand the LLP's current state and planned actions—as crucial for building trust[Xiaolu Bai et al., 2024].

Trust and perceived control emerge as recurring themes across user experience findings, particularly in control customisation contexts. Resnik et al.'s qualitative study of the DEKA Arm with EMG-PR control found that the majority of participants preferred their personal prosthetic controls over the EMG-PR system, citing complexity, calibration burden, and fatigue as primary concerns[L. Resnik et al., 2018]; most were nonetheless optimistic about the future potential of EMG-PR as a method[L. Resnik et al., 2018]. This pattern—scepticism toward current implementation alongside openness to the underlying technology—recurs in several studies. The Mbithi et al. qualitative interviews found that while EG sockets were statistically non-inferior in comfort, participants valued expert prosthetist input and saw co-design with the clinician as meaningful in itself[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024]; the platform could not substitute for the relational and communicative functions of clinical interaction. The Bai et al. review of intelligent LLP interaction similarly identifies the wearer's need to understand the device's goals and behaviour as a prerequisite for trust, and notes that prior knowledge of the UCI significantly affects the wearer's ability to interact with it productively[Xiaolu Bai et al., 2024].

The intelligibility of automated recommendations is addressed most directly in the Alili et al. study, which found that users required additional explanation of knee kinematics to operate the interface effectively[Abbas Alili et al., 2023], and in the Sungeelee et al. study, where the learner-led training strategy improved users' mental models of the classifier's recognition behaviour[Vaynee Sungeelee et al., 2024]. The Reviewer system of Yang et al. was specifically designed to address intelligibility by making the classifier's decision space directly visible, and demonstrated that structured feedback reduces reliance on heuristic recalibration[Ruichen Yang et al., 2025]. These findings collectively suggest that intelligibility of platform logic—rather than merely usability of the interface—is a distinct and important dimension of user experience in control customisation systems.

In physical design contexts, intelligibility concerns arise differently. Eshraghi et al. found that clinicians performing digital rectification reported challenges due to the absence of tactile feedback, which in traditional casting allows prosthetists to detect tissue compliance and apply clinical judgment in a fundamentally embodied way[A. Eshraghi et al., 2024]; this represents a form of intelligibility deficit created by digitisation itself, in which the platform's abstractions are less interpretable to experts than the physical material it replaces. Olsen et al. similarly found that the absence of clinical experience in a research-led socket fabrication process led to specific quality failures, and recommended that algorithmic tools such as FEA be used to partially compensate for missing tacit knowledge[Jennifer Olsen et al., 2020].

## Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1: The Platform as Structured Professional Tool vs. Neutral Interface

The evidence across the corpus converges on a clear finding: digital prosthetic customisation platforms are not neutral interfaces but structured collaboration spaces that encode existing professional hierarchies and workflows into their architecture. The SMA and SMA² systems were explicitly designed to replicate the operations of orthopaedic technicians using virtual tools[Stella Gabbiadini, 2011; G. Colombo et al., 2018], making the technician's knowledge and practice the organising principle of the interface. The knowledge-based framework of Gabbiadini embeds expert knowledge via virtual assistants that provide structured workflows and automated assistance, with no direct evidence of end-user involvement in the design process[Stella Gabbiadini, 2011]. The clinician control interfaces of Wells et al.[D. Wells et al., 2002], Quintero et al.[David Quintero et al., 2018], and Reznick et al.[Emma Reznick et al., 2024] similarly position the clinical professional as the authoritative configurator, with the platform functioning as a translator of clinical judgment into device behaviour.

This professional-centric structuring is not uniformly problematic. Several studies demonstrate that embedding clinical knowledge into platform architecture improves design quality and reproducibility. The Lee et al. physics-based digital framework achieved statistically significant improvements in comfort and pain reduction relative to conventional artisanal methods[Duncan R. C. Lee et al., 2024], suggesting that algorithmic encoding of biomechanical principles can outperform unaided clinical judgment in specific measurable dimensions. The PACT algorithm's ability to deliver a clinically-tolerant first-draft socket in 13 seconds[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026] illustrates the efficiency gains possible when structured professional knowledge is successfully formalised and automated. However, both systems also identify residual roles for clinical expertise: PACT's authors recommend incorporating metadata such as tissue stiffness, age, and clinician-led measurements to improve personalisation[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026], while Lee et al. note that manual modifications remain part of the workflow.

Where the professional-centric model encounters its limits is in contexts where end-user needs, values, or preferences cannot be adequately proxied by professional knowledge. The Mbithi et al. qualitative data capture participants expressing the value of expert prosthetist input in terms that go beyond technical quality—they describe the fitting session itself, and the clinician's communicative engagement, as a component of their experience[F. Mbithi et al., 2025]. The Whatley et al. and Zhou et al. aesthetic co-design systems reveal a dimension of prosthetic customisation—identity, expressiveness, agency over bodily appearance—that professional-centric design platforms systematically under-serve[Sarah Whatley et al., 2023; Feng Zhou et al., 2023]. The disabled dancers in those studies reported a strong sense of ownership over designs derived from their movements[Sarah Whatley et al., 2023], a form of perceived control unavailable in conventional clinical design workflows.

### Theme 2: Automation and the Redistribution of Expertise

A second theme running through the corpus concerns the way increasing automation redistributes expertise rather than simply replacing it. The SMA series illustrates this: initial versions required high levels of tacit knowledge from prosthetists; successive versions incorporated more automated steps (MRI reconstruction, automatic rectification, FEA-based optimisation), while new forms of expertise—in operating the digital interface, interpreting simulation outputs, and using hand-tracking devices—were required in return[A. Vitali, 2016; G. Colombo et al., 2018; R. Morotti, 2014]. This is consistent with the Eshraghi et al. finding that clinicians performing digital rectification required adaptation to compensate for the absence of tactile feedback[A. Eshraghi et al., 2024; A. Eshraghi et al., 2024], and with the Howard et al. finding that non-expert designers require explicit training in digital design and manufacturing to participate in co-design workflows[J. Howard et al., 2024].

In control customisation systems, automation acts primarily as a cognitive offload mechanism for end users, reducing the number of degrees of freedom that must be actively managed. The semi-autonomous gaze-EMG interface of Greene et al. demonstrated that when users can command a prosthesis in task-space rather than joint-space, they show significantly reduced compensatory motion of the upper arm (p ≤ 0.05), more effective utilisation of configuration space (p ≤ 0.01), and more efficient motion (p ≤ 0.01) compared with traditional classification-based interfaces[Rebecca J. Greene et al., 2024]. The Cirelli et al. context-aware semiautonomous system, which combines user-initiated grasp selection via EMG with computer vision for wrist orientation and grasp refinement, achieved a 100% success rate (vs. 97.69% for EMG-alone) while reducing task completion time by up to 52.66% and improving system usability scale scores by 15 points[Gianmarco Cirelli et al., 2026]. The Stracquadanio et al. eye-in-hand shared-autonomy system similarly enabled faster grasps and simplified user experience relative to multi-DOF manual control[Giuseppe Stracquadanio et al., 2025]. These systems position automation not as a replacement for user agency but as a mechanism for making that agency more effective, an architecture the Chunhao Peng et al. system makes explicit by preserving user authority (via EMG and voice) while allowing autonomous decision-making for speed and efficiency[Chunhao Peng et al., 2025].

The Marković sensor-fusion framework provides a theoretical grounding for this redistribution: by equipping the prosthetic hand with computer vision and inertial sensing, the controller takes over contextual adjustment (hand preshaping, orientation) while the user provides only high-level commands via a two-channel myoelectric interface[Marko Marković, 2016; Marko Marković, 2016]. This architecture simplifies the user's control problem not by reducing the prosthesis's capability but by shifting lower-level decisions to the automated layer. The resulting system is described as both more usable and more capable—an important empirical claim that challenges the assumption that automation necessarily diminishes user control.

However, the redistribution of expertise through automation creates new forms of dependence and new failure modes. Several sources identify the training data dependency as a critical limitation. PACT's age-related biases arise from the distribution of its training library[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026]. Evidence-generated socket systems trained on single-centre datasets may not generalise to different clinical populations[F. Mbithi et al., 2025]. The Resnik et al. study found that the specific iteration of EMG-PR used with the DEKA Arm created problems of complexity and calibration that outweighed the system's theoretical benefits for most participants[L. Resnik et al., 2018], suggesting that automation that requires more user effort to maintain than it saves in operation is unlikely to be adopted. Bai et al.'s framework for intelligent LLP interaction identifies this as a systemic issue: prior knowledge of the UCI affects the wearer's ability to interact productively with the device, and interfaces designed without attention to the learning curve may create barriers rather than reduce them[Xiaolu Bai et al., 2024; Xiaolu Bai et al., 2024].

### Theme 3: Co-design as Aspiration and Practice

A third theme concerns the gap between co-design as a stated aspiration and co-design as a practically implemented process. Several sources describe co-design processes in ways that involve genuine bidirectional influence between professionals and users, while others use the language of co-design to describe processes that are primarily consultative.

The most developed co-design implementations in the corpus involve aesthetic and identity-related customisation. The Whatley et al. and Zhou et al. projects embedded disabled dancers directly in the generative algorithm's input loop, with the dancers' movements constituting the primary design data[Sarah Whatley et al., 2023; Feng Zhou et al., 2023]. The Vlachostergiou Aquaborg project iteratively developed speculative wearable designs through co-creative sessions between the researcher-designer and a single female prosthetic user, explicitly attending to the user's affective and emotional needs alongside physical requirements[Maria Vlachostergiou, 2022; Maria Vlachostergiou, 2022]. The Lin et al. VR collaborative design study used the Collab feature of Gravity Sketch to enable simultaneous modification of virtual prosthetic models by designers, medical technicians, and users[Ming-Huang Lin, 2023], with positive assessments from both user participants and prosthesis designers[Ming-Huang Lin, 2023].

In clinical and functional customisation contexts, co-design is more typically operationalised as iterative user feedback within a clinician-led design process. The Mbithi et al. studies describe clinicians as responsible for design decisions while patients provide comfort ratings and participate in selection between alternatives[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024]. The Howard et al. workshop study identifies barriers to genuine co-design scaling within healthcare services, noting that end-users must be engaged from the outset to set goals and manage expectations, but that current systems lack the digital infrastructure to make feedback easily accessible and actionable across the service ecosystem[J. Howard et al., 2024]. The study maps sixteen individual stakeholder categories and ten corporate stakeholder groups as part of the AT co-design ecosystem[J. Howard et al., 2024], underscoring the structural complexity of implementing symmetric co-design in clinical environments.

The Peixoto et al. platform demonstrates that co-design is more tractable when it is separated from clinical function and focused on personalisation at the level of aesthetics and identity[Sara Peixoto et al., 2025]. By decoupling aesthetic customisation from clinical fitting, the platform creates a domain where users can exercise genuine autonomy without requiring clinical supervision—though the authors acknowledge that the platform's current scope is limited to surface customisation[Sara Peixoto et al., 2025].

### Theme 4: Access, Equity, and Scalability

A final theme concerns the relationship between platform design and the equitable distribution of prosthetic access. Several sources explicitly frame digital manufacturing and platform-mediated customisation as potential solutions to accessibility inequities. The De Vivo Nicoloso et al. LIMBER workflow for 3D-printed monocoque transtibial prostheses reports reductions in average cost, weight, and production time by 95%, 55%, and 95% respectively compared with traditional devices[Luca Gabriele De Vivo Nicoloso et al., 2021], with the authors identifying rural and under-resourced communities as primary beneficiaries. The Junghun Lee et al. computer-vision-based system for resource-constrained environments uses a mobile phone and open-source software to create virtual limb models and generate customised prosthetic hands with minimal specialist involvement[Junghun Lee et al., 2023; Junghun Lee et al., 2023], directly targeting the barrier of specialist unavailability. The Romero et al. affordable AI-driven myoelectric prosthesis achieved an average BAM-ULA score of 7.4 out of 10 across nine transradial amputees and demonstrated performance comparable to high-end commercial devices[Enzo Romero et al., 2025], with the authors explicitly addressing the needs of users in low-income regions.

The Oldfrey et al. scoping review of digital fabrication in prosthetics provides a broader perspective on this theme, noting that while digital technologies offer reductions in manual labour and potential for automation, the field is dominated by small, proof-of-concept studies using FDM techniques, with few formal studies on mature technologies, little discussion of training requirements for personnel, and a lack of standardised testing methodologies[B. Oldfrey et al., 2024]. The review identifies the absence of long-term, large-scale studies as a critical gap, and calls for greater collaboration between academia, industry, and clinical teams to move from proof-of-concept to evidence-based implementation[B. Oldfrey et al., 2024].

## Synthesis

### The Apparent Contradiction: Automation vs. Retained Clinical Authority

The corpus presents a surface-level contradiction. On one hand, multiple studies demonstrate that automated platform-generated designs are statistically comparable to clinician-generated designs in comfort and fit outcomes (Mbithi et al. EG sockets, Lee et al. FEA interfaces, Pendse PACT algorithm), implying that clinical expertise may be partially substitutable by algorithmic logic. On the other hand, qualitative findings from Mbithi et al.[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024], Eshraghi et al.[A. Eshraghi et al., 2024], and Howard et al.[J. Howard et al., 2024] consistently show that clinical expertise is not merely a technical resource but also a relational, communicative, and contextually adaptive function that platforms have not yet reproduced.

These two findings are reconcilable. The quantitative non-inferiority results are aggregate findings: across a population of users, algorithm-generated sockets perform comparably to clinician-led designs on average. But average performance conceals the distribution of individual outcomes. The EG socket studies report lower variability in comfort scores[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024]—a finding that cuts both ways. Reduced variability means fewer poor outcomes, but it may also mean fewer optimised outcomes for individuals with unusual anatomy or complex clinical needs. The PACT algorithm's age-related bias toward undersizing paediatric and oversizing adult sockets[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026] is precisely the kind of systematic error that aggregate non-inferiority can mask. Clinical expertise may be most valuable not at the average but at the tails of the distribution, where patients differ most markedly from the training data underlying algorithmic systems.

This framing resolves the contradiction: automation effectively handles baseline design for the modal patient, while clinical expertise remains essential for atypical cases, relational communication, and the integration of contextual information (tissue health, psychological state, activity goals) that algorithms do not currently incorporate. The appropriate architecture is therefore not automation replacing the clinician but automation restructuring the clinician's work, moving their contribution from routine design to high-value customisation and patient collaboration—which is precisely the design intention articulated in the Mbithi et al. series[F. Mbithi et al., 2025; F. Mbithi et al., 2024] and the PACT algorithm[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026].

### Why Control Interface Studies and Socket Design Studies Diverge

A second tension in the corpus concerns the different patterns of end-user participation found in socket design versus control customisation research. End users are largely absent as configurative agents in socket design platforms but are progressively central in control customisation systems. This divergence reflects genuine differences in the nature of the configuration task rather than differing philosophies of user involvement.

Socket geometry is determined by tissue biomechanics, load transfer requirements, and residual limb anatomy—domains in which the patient lacks the technical knowledge to make informed parametric adjustments without clinical mediation. Control preference, by contrast, is inherently subjective: the user alone knows how a given control mapping feels during task performance. The Alili et al. blinded study provides direct evidence for this, showing that users can reliably identify their preferred control profiles when presented with alternatives[Abbas Alili et al., 2023], while their clinicians cannot make that determination on their behalf. Similarly, Arunachalam et al. propose that control optimisation should be structured around the user's own cost functions (effort, time, reliability) rather than a clinician-defined objective[A. Arunachalam et al., 2019; A. Arunachalam et al., 2019], and Garcia-Rosas et al. demonstrate that implicit human motor behaviour—specifically upper-body compensation, measurable via wearable IMUs—can serve as an objective function for automatic prosthesis personalisation without requiring explicit user feedback[Ricardo Garcia-Rosas et al., 2020; Ricardo Garcia-Rosas et al., 2020].

These findings collectively suggest that the appropriate boundary between clinical authority and user authority differs by design domain. In socket geometry, clinical authority should dominate with user input as a comfort check. In control preference, user authority should dominate with clinical oversight ensuring safety and technical feasibility. Current platforms that apply a single collaboration model across both domains are likely to either over-involve users in technical decisions they cannot meaningfully evaluate or under-involve them in preference decisions that only they can make.

### The Evidence Gap for Upper-Limb Socket Design

The most significant structural gap in the corpus concerns upper-limb socket design specifically. While there is substantial evidence from lower-limb socket design platforms (the Colombo/Vitali/Gabbiadini/Morotti series, the Mbithi evidence-generated socket studies, the Lee FEA framework, the Bonacini and Buldo methodology), analogous digital platforms for trans-radial, trans-humeral, or more proximal upper-limb sockets are represented by only a few sources: the Olsen et al. study of a research-led trans-radial socket fabrication process[Jennifer Olsen et al., 2020], the Eshraghi et al. feasibility study of digital vs. manual socket design for below-knee amputees (relevant by analogy)[A. Eshraghi et al., 2024], the Pendse PACT algorithm for transradial sockets[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026], and the Binedell et al. forequarter prosthesis case study[Trevor Binedell et al., 2020]. Olsen et al. explicitly note that modernising upper-limb prosthetic socket manufacturing has seen limited progress, with casting techniques resembling those developed over 50 years ago[Jennifer Olsen et al., 2020], and the Oldfrey et al. scoping review identifies a lack of long-term large-scale studies as a critical limitation of the current digital fabrication evidence base[B. Oldfrey et al., 2024].

This gap is consequential for the research question. Upper-limb socket fit has different biomechanical constraints from lower-limb fit, with different pressure tolerances, different functional demands during manipulation tasks, and different relationships between socket geometry and prosthetic control signal quality. Whether the design paradigms, parametric tools, FEA models, and evidence-generation methods developed for lower-limb sockets transfer to upper-limb contexts is an empirical question that the literature has not yet adequately addressed. The PACT algorithm's results for transradial sockets[Vishal Pendse et al., 2026] represent a beginning, but with n = 19 and identified age-related biases, it does not yet constitute mature evidence. Future work that directly resolves this gap would require prospective clinical studies comparing digital and traditional socket design approaches for upper-limb amputees using comfort, control quality, and functional performance as joint outcomes—a study design not currently represented in the corpus.

The Yang et al. Reviewer system demonstrates how platforms control visibility to shape outcomes: "The Reviewer system provides a clear map between muscle contractions and their resultant position within the decision-space" while the control group used "virtual arm interface without direct visibility of decision boundaries" . Making classifier boundaries visible led to "higher completion rates, reduced overshoot, and improved path efficiency," showing that **what platforms make visible directly affects user capability**.

**Parametric Constraint Architecture:**
The Alili et al. user-controlled interface illustrates constrained agency: users could "select their preferred knee kinematics in a 4-dimensional space by modifying control points" while "the low-level RL-based tuning algorithm adjusting all 12 impedance control parameters" . Users have **apparent control** over high-level preferences while the platform **mediates the translation** to technical parameters.

**Decision Authority Distribution:**
The clinical tuning interfaces show how platforms redistribute decision-making. The Reznick et al. CTI "translates common clinical tuning parameters into model adjustments" enabling prosthetists to complete "full multi-activity prosthesis controller in under 20 min" . The platform **amplifies professional capability** while **constraining the parameter space** to clinically interpretable dimensions.

## Platform-Mediated Variant Comparison

**Structured Comparison Mechanisms:**
The Mbithi et al. crossover studies demonstrate how platforms structure variant comparison: patients were "blinded to which socket was prosthetist-designed or evidence-generated" while prosthetists could compare both approaches . The platform **controls the comparison context** and **determines what alternatives** are available for evaluation.

**Algorithmic Variant Generation:**
The PACT algorithm shows automated variant creation: it "retrieves the most similar limb–socket pair from a reference library and applies isotropic and anisotropic scaling adjustments" . Rather than presenting all possible designs, the platform **curates the design space** based on algorithmic similarity measures.

## Differentiated Professional Experiences

**Expertise Amplification vs. Constraint:**
The Eshraghi et al. study revealed how digitization can simultaneously enhance and constrain professional capability. While digital scanning provided "higher satisfaction" and "on-screen visualization," clinicians reported "challenges due to lack of tactile feedback" which "makes the modifications of the digitally-scanned residuum more challenging" . The platform **trades certain forms of professional knowledge** (tactile) for others (visual, computational).

**Training and Supervision Integration:**
The Rozevink et al. VR training study showed that "therapists scored significantly higher in conscious processing and visual attention" compared to prosthesis users , but both operated the same interface. The platform **accommodates different cognitive approaches** while maintaining unified functionality.

## Evidence Gaps and Limitations

**Upper-Limb Socket Design Mediation:**
The report identifies that "the most significant structural gap in the corpus concerns upper-limb socket design specifically." How platforms mediate socket design for trans-radial and trans-humeral applications—including supervision mechanisms, constraint-setting, and professional role differentiation—remains largely unknown.

**Long-term Mediation Effects:**
The Oldfrey et al. review found "very little formal discussion on the training of personnel on using the equipment" . How platform-mediated workflows change professional expertise, decision-making patterns, and collaboration dynamics over extended periods is not well documented.

## Synthesis

Digital prosthetic platforms function as **active design mediators** rather than neutral tools. They structure asymmetric collaboration through differentiated interfaces, embed supervision through automated guidance and constraint-setting, and shape design outcomes by controlling what users can see, modify, and decide. The platforms **redistribute agency** rather than simply digitizing existing processes—amplifying certain forms of professional expertise while constraining others, enabling new forms of user participation while limiting decision authority, and creating new supervision mechanisms while potentially reducing traditional oversight capabilities. This mediation is most sophisticated in lower-limb socket design and control customization contexts, with significant gaps remaining in upper-limb socket design applications.

### Q5 What are the most important literature gaps in this report regarding upper-limb socket customization platforms, especially for comparing professional experience and end-user experience within the same system?

Based on the research report, the most critical literature gaps regarding upper-limb socket customization platforms center on the **fundamental absence of comparative studies** examining professional versus end-user experiences within the same digital system. The report identifies this as "the most significant structural gap in the corpus."

## Primary Gap: Absence of Upper-Limb Socket Design Platforms

**Complete Evidence Void:**
While the report contains substantial evidence from lower-limb socket design platforms (the Colombo/SMA² series, Mbithi evidence-generated sockets, Morotti virtual testing laboratory), there are virtually **no analogous digital platforms for trans-radial, trans-humeral, or more proximal upper-limb socket design**. The few relevant studies include:

- The Olsen et al. research-led trans-radial socket fabrication process 
- The PACT algorithm for transradial sockets with n=19 and identified age-related biases  
- The Binedell et al. forequarter prosthesis case study 

**Critical Knowledge Gap:**
The Olsen study explicitly notes that "modernising upper-limb prosthetic socket manufacturing has seen limited progress, with casting techniques resembling those developed over 50 years ago" . Whether the sophisticated design paradigms, parametric tools, FEA models, and evidence-generation methods developed for lower-limb contexts transfer to upper-limb biomechanical and functional requirements remains **completely unknown**.

## Missing Comparative Professional-User Experience Studies

**No Within-System Comparisons:**
The report reveals that **no studies directly compare how technical professionals and end users experience the same upper-limb socket customization platform**. This represents a fundamental gap because:

- **Different Biomechanical Constraints**: Upper-limb socket fit has different pressure tolerances, functional demands during manipulation tasks, and relationships between socket geometry and prosthetic control signal quality compared to lower-limb applications
- **Different Professional Workflows**: Upper-limb prosthetists may have different expertise patterns, decision-making processes, and collaboration needs than lower-limb specialists
- **Different User Priorities**: Upper-limb prosthesis users may prioritize different aspects of fit, comfort, and functionality that affect their platform interaction patterns

**Asymmetric Collaboration Architecture Unknown:**
The report demonstrates that lower-limb platforms consistently organize "asymmetric collaboration with clearly differentiated roles and capabilities" . Whether this pattern holds for upper-limb socket design, and how the asymmetry might differ, is completely unexplored.

## Specific Missing Evidence Categories

**1. Professional Adaptation and Confidence:**
The Eshraghi et al. study revealed that clinicians performing digital socket rectification reported "challenges due to lack of tactile feedback" which "makes the modifications of the digitally-scanned residuum more challenging for clinicians as their confidence in applying the biomechanical principals is hindered" . **No equivalent studies exist for upper-limb socket design professionals**.

**2. Supervision and Constraint-Setting Mechanisms:**
Lower-limb platforms demonstrate sophisticated supervision through automated flagging (pressure visualization in FEA systems) and constraint-setting by design. The Bai et al. framework shows how "the tuning interface could allow LLP wearers to adjust a few of the most essential control parameters, and the remaining settings are only accessible by prosthetists" . **No evidence exists about how such mechanisms would function in upper-limb socket design contexts**.

**3. End-User Configurative Agency:**
The report shows that in control customization, end users demonstrate meaningful authority (Alili et al. user-controlled interfaces, Hepp et al. smartphone systems), while in lower-limb socket design, users primarily serve as "data sources and comfort evaluators." **How end users would participate in upper-limb socket configuration is unknown**.

**4. Platform-Mediated Variant Comparison:**
The Mbithi et al. crossover studies demonstrate sophisticated variant comparison mechanisms for lower-limb sockets. **No studies examine how professionals and users would compare upper-limb socket variants** or what comparison criteria would be prioritized.

## Methodological Gaps

**Lack of Longitudinal Studies:**
The Oldfrey et al. scoping review found "a lack of appropriate long-term, large-scale studies" with "few studies had N > 5" . This is particularly problematic for upper-limb socket design where **adaptation patterns, professional learning curves, and user acceptance may differ significantly** from lower-limb contexts.

**Missing Training and Professional Development Evidence:**
The report notes "very little formal discussion on the training of personnel on using the equipment, even for established digital fabrication technologies" . For upper-limb socket design, **how professionals would learn to use digital platforms, what training would be required, and how expertise would transfer** is completely unexplored.

**Absence of Cross-Platform Professional Experience Studies:**
**No studies compare technical professionals' experiences across different platform types** (socket design vs. control customization vs. training systems) to understand how professional roles and confidence vary by application domain in upper-limb contexts.

## Critical Research Needs

**1. Foundational Platform Development Studies:**
Prospective clinical studies comparing digital and traditional socket design approaches for upper-limb amputees using comfort, control quality, and functional performance as joint outcomes.

**2. Comparative Professional-User Experience Research:**
Within-system studies examining how prosthetists, technicians, and end users experience the same upper-limb socket customization platform, with particular attention to role differentiation, decision-making authority, and collaboration dynamics.

**3. Biomechanical Transferability Studies:**
Research determining whether lower-limb design paradigms, parametric tools, and evidence-generation methods are appropriate for upper-limb biomechanical constraints and functional requirements.

**4. Long-term Adaptation Studies:**
Longitudinal research examining how professional workflows, user acceptance, and collaboration patterns evolve with extended use of upper-limb socket customization platforms.

This evidence gap is particularly consequential because upper-limb prosthetics may require fundamentally different approaches to digital customization, professional-user collaboration, and platform-mediated design processes than the lower-limb paradigms that currently dominate the literature.
