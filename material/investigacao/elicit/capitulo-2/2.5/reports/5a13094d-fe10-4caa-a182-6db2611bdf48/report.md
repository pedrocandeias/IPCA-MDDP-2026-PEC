## Summary

The literature consistently establishes that AI—spanning decision support, optimization, computer vision, natural language processing, and generative systems—augments rather than replaces the industrial designer across all process stages, serving as a powerful assistive tool for parametric customization and assistive product development while requiring sustained human oversight to manage inherent risks of bias, opacity, error, and creative homogenization.

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence is best understood as a nested hierarchy: AI is the umbrella term for computational techniques that approximate cognitive functions such as learning and pattern recognition; machine learning is the sub-discipline that trains algorithms from examples; deep learning uses artificial neural networks to mimic brain-like learning; and generative AI applies deep learning to produce novel creative outputs from user prompts[K. Thoring et al., 2023; Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024]. Five forms of AI are most relevant to design practice—decision support, optimization, computer vision, natural language processing, and generative systems—with generative tools (e.g., DALL-E, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion) dominating recent attention[Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024; Xiang Liu, 2026], while optimization and parametric systems (e.g., Finch 3D, Autodesk Generative Design) and NLP-based interfaces are particularly salient for parametric customization and assistive product development[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025; Mr. Udit Gupta et al., 2025; Samuelle Bourgault & J. E, 2023]. AI contributions map onto recognized design-process stages: broadening ideation through rapid variant generation[Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025; Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026], supporting problem framing through data analysis[Y. Lai et al., 2023; Qian Huang & King Wang Poon, 2026], accelerating prototyping through simulation and sketch-to-mockup translation[Thijs Willems et al., 2025; A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025], and enhancing evaluation through performance prediction and user testing[Xiang Liu, 2026; Gerry Derksen & Sejal Bansal, 2024]. AI is consistently strongest in divergent phases, while convergent selection and contextual interpretation require human judgment[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026; K. Thoring et al., 2023].

The literature is unanimous that AI functions as an assistive tool rather than an autonomous substitute for the designer, whose irreducible contributions include problem definition, cultural sensitivity, ethical reasoning, and the integration of tacit knowledge that AI cannot access[Xiang Liu, 2026; R. H. Wardi et al., 2025; Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025; Qian Huang & King Wang Poon, 2026]. Documented risks include bias from skewed training data[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026; Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024], opacity of neural-network decision-making[K. Thoring et al., 2023; Mr. Udit Gupta et al., 2025], hallucination and unreliable outputs[K. Thoring et al., 2023; Thijs Willems et al., 2025], creative atrophy and stylistic homogenization from overreliance[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026; Olena Shabanova, 2025], and unresolved questions of authorship and originality[Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024; Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025]. Mitigation strategies include deploying AI in divergent exploration while retaining human veto in convergent selection[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026], structured role-reflection protocols that reward deliberate non-use[Thijs Willems et al., 2025], and cultivating AI literacy that encompasses technical competence, ethical reasoning, and critical evaluation[T. H. Teo & Dexter X. H. Chew, 2026]. For parametric customization and assistive product development, the evidence supports a layered integration in which optimization systems handle constraint-based exploration, NLP interfaces lower barriers for non-specialist users, generative tools accelerate visual communication, and the designer retains strategic and ethical oversight throughout[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025; Samuelle Bourgault & J. E, 2023; Chang Xiong et al., 2026; Xiang Liu, 2026].

## Thematic Analysis

### Defining Artificial Intelligence and Its Sub-fields

A foundational requirement for introducing AI to non-specialist readers is a clear definitional hierarchy. Across the corpus, AI is most frequently described as a family of computational techniques that approximate cognitive functions—learning, reasoning, and pattern recognition—through data-driven models[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026]. It is also characterized as a domain within computer science that aims to understand and replicate human intelligence in computational systems[Züleyha Değirmenci, 2024]. One source offers a further categorical distinction: AI can be divided into machines that mimic human thought processes (cognitive modeling and the Turing test approach) and rational machines guided by laws of thought and a rational-agent approach[Züleyha Değirmenci, 2024].

The nested relationship among AI sub-fields is clarified most explicitly by Thoring et al., who define AI as an umbrella term for machines that can perceive, synthesize, and infer information; machine learning as a sub-discipline that trains algorithms with examples to make predictions; deep learning as a subset of machine learning using artificial neural networks to mimic human brain learning; and generative AI as systems that use deep learning algorithms to generate creative outputs based on given inputs or prompts[K. Thoring et al., 2023]. Bartlett and Camba add a complementary process-level explanation: image-generative AI models are trained on large datasets of images paired with textual descriptions and work through a process called diffusion—adding noise to data and then denoising it to generate images based on user prompts[Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024]. Xiong et al. offer a concise working definition of generative AI (GenAI) as a class of AI systems capable of autonomously generating new content such as images, texts, and design solutions based on learned data patterns[Chang Xiong et al., 2026].

Several sources position AI not merely as a technical capability but as a relational concept within design practice. Liu describes it as a collaborative intelligent agent that intervenes in spatial cognition and design judgment[Xiang Liu, 2026]. Wardi et al. frame AI through Reciprocal Human-Machine Learning (RHML), emphasizing dynamic two-way learning between human designers and AI systems[R. H. Wardi et al., 2025]. Wong notes that AI is good at completing discrete tasks such as scheduling an appointment but incapable of more general intellectual tasks such as knowing when to reschedule[J. S. Wong, 2018], a distinction that remains relevant for calibrating non-specialist expectations.

Generative systems receive the most attention across the corpus, consistent with the post-2022 surge in large-model capabilities. However, the optimization and NLP categories are particularly salient for parametric customization: parametric systems like Finch 3D automate spatial layout generation and accelerate work with design variations[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025], while NLP-based interfaces allow designers to interact through semantic descriptions rather than technical commands[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025]. For assistive product development, decision-support systems that translate constraints for non-specialists are noted as making parametric customization more accessible[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026], and tools like CADgpt lower language and technical barriers for inclusive education[Samuelle Bourgault & J. E, 2023].

### AI Integration Across Design Process Stages

The sources map AI contributions onto recognized design-process models, most frequently the double-diamond (discover–define–develop–deliver) framework. The pattern that emerges is one of stage-specific augmentation rather than wholesale automation.

A consistent finding is that AI contributions are strongest in divergent phases—broadening the space of possibilities through rapid generation of alternatives—while convergent phases demand human judgment for selection, synthesis, and contextual interpretation[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026; K. Thoring et al., 2023; Qian Huang & King Wang Poon, 2026]. Derksen and Bansal, working with design students, extended the double-diamond into a "triple diamond" model to accommodate iterative prompt refinement and user testing as distinct stages of divergence and convergence[Gerry Derksen & Sejal Bansal, 2024]. Laugier observes a more fundamental epistemological shift: generative AI moves the design process from an "écriture des formes" (writing of forms through informed drawing) to an "exercice d'un regard" (exercise of a gaze), reframing the designer's cognitive engagement with form[Sonia Laugier, 2025].

For parametric customization specifically, Finch 3D exemplifies how AI automates spatial layout generation, enabling students and practitioners to quickly generate and compare parametric variants[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025]. Autodesk's Generative Design and Spacemaker AI produce multiple design iterations based on given parameters, supporting optimization against structural, energy, and sustainability criteria[Mr. Udit Gupta et al., 2025]. The Zhitu AI platform demonstrates how GenAI-based tools can support rapid image generation and secondary editing tailored to enterprise-specific scenarios[Chang Xiong et al., 2026].

### The Designer-AI Relationship: Assistive Tool, Not Autonomous Substitute

The most robust consensus across the entire corpus is that AI should function as an assistive tool rather than an autonomous replacement for human designers. Every source that addresses the designer-AI relationship explicitly or implicitly supports this position[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026; Salvatore Di Dio et al., 2024; Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024; T. H. Teo & Dexter X. H. Chew, 2026; K. Thoring et al., 2023; Xiang Liu, 2026; Züleyha Değirmenci, 2024; Olena Shabanova, 2025; Chang Xiong et al., 2026; R. H. Wardi et al., 2025; Mirko Stanimirović et al., 2026; Mr. Udit Gupta et al., 2025; Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025; Thijs Willems et al., 2025]. Several distinct models of the human-AI relationship have been proposed.

The **hybrid intelligence** model positions the designer as a mediator between algorithmic precision and human needs, acting as what Di Dio et al. term a "sense maker" who infuses meaning and context into AI-generated outputs[Salvatore Di Dio et al., 2024]. Mirzaei et al. recommend allocating divergent exploration primarily to AI while retaining human judgment for convergent selection and ethical decision-making[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026]. Liu describes three evolving architectural roles: from form-makers to creative directors who curate prompts, to system integrators who orchestrate cross-platform workflows, to ethical supervisors responsible for contextual adequacy and social impact[Xiang Liu, 2026].

The **reciprocal learning** model, advanced by Wardi et al., frames the relationship through RHML in which dynamic, two-way learning between human designers and AI systems occurs, with the Polanyi Paradox (humans know more than they can tell) and Algorithm Aversion serving as key mediating constructs[R. H. Wardi et al., 2025]. This model emphasizes that AI cannot access the tacit knowledge embedded in design expertise.

The **role-tagging** model from Willems et al. provides an educational taxonomy distinguishing AI as tool (instrumental assistant for efficiency), teammate (collaborative partner for complex problem-solving), or neither (deliberate non-use when human presence or empathy is required)[Thijs Willems et al., 2025]. Students in their 500-person course learned not only to harness AI for speed but also to reject its outputs and divert reclaimed time into deeper user research[Thijs Willems et al., 2025].

The **cognitive accelerator** framing from Huang and Poon is especially relevant to the assistive-tool thesis: generative AI functions primarily as a cognitive accelerator rather than a replacement for human creativity, with students consistently relying on human judgment to interpret contextual information and validate AI-generated outputs[Qian Huang & King Wang Poon, 2026]. Domain knowledge derived from field observations enabled students to detect inaccuracies in AI suggestions[Qian Huang & King Wang Poon, 2026].

Across these models, the designer competencies required for effective AI collaboration converge on several themes: prompt engineering and communication skills[K. Thoring et al., 2023; Chang Xiong et al., 2026; A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025]; data literacy and bias auditing[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026]; critical evaluation and interpretation of algorithmic outputs[Olena Shabanova, 2025; Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025]; ethical reasoning[T. H. Teo & Dexter X. H. Chew, 2026; Xiang Liu, 2026]; and the human capacities of Agency, Domain Knowledge, Imagination, and Taste identified in the SuperSkillsStack framework[Qian Huang & King Wang Poon, 2026]. Chaca Espinoza synthesizes this evolution by describing the industrial designer as no longer substituted by AI but redefined as an integrator of multiple intelligences, whose work requires new technical, ethical, and reflexive competencies[Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025].

### Educational Approaches to AI Literacy in Design

Several sources offer pedagogical models that are directly relevant to writing an accessible introductory section for non-specialist readers. Teo and Chew propose a layered framework for AI literacy in design intelligence comprising five strata: foundational knowledge (data, algorithms, models, computation), AI literacy competencies (technical, critical, ethical, adaptive), design thinking methods, human-AI collaboration practices, and outcome application domains[T. H. Teo & Dexter X. H. Chew, 2026]. The SuperSkillsStack framework identifies four key human competencies—Agency, Domain Knowledge, Imagination, and Taste—that must be cultivated alongside AI tools for effective collaboration[Qian Huang & King Wang Poon, 2026]. Liu recommends a triadic pedagogical system of "algorithmic understanding–spatial control–ethical governance" for integrating AI into architectural education[Xiang Liu, 2026].

In practice-based education, Willems et al. introduced a three-way taxonomy (tool, teammate, neither) that enabled over 500 first-year students to develop metacognitive awareness about when AI should and should not be employed[Thijs Willems et al., 2025]. Troshkin and Makukhin demonstrated that comparing students' own spatial solutions with algorithmically generated alternatives enhanced critical thinking and improved design decision quality[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025]. Stanimirović et al. propose "AI sparring" as a pedagogical framework in which AI serves not as a helper but as a provocateur, pushing students toward deeper critical reflection and stronger concept development[Mirko Stanimirović et al., 2026]. Bartlett and Camba recommend that educators introduce AI as one tool among many, require documentation of AI use, ensure students can complete tasks without AI, and engage students in discussions of ethical issues including bias, artist theft, and copyright[Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024].

These educational frameworks collectively reinforce the view that accessible AI introductions should not merely explain what AI does technically but should simultaneously equip readers to think critically about when, why, and how AI is appropriately deployed in design—and when it should be deliberately set aside.

## Synthesis

The 25 sources converge strongly on the fundamental characterization of AI as a family of computational techniques for pattern recognition, learning, and generation that augments rather than replaces human design intelligence. The definitional hierarchy—AI as umbrella term, machine learning as the training paradigm, deep learning as the neural-network implementation, and generative AI as the creative-output application layer[K. Thoring et al., 2023]—is consistent across the sources that address it, providing a reliable scaffold for non-specialist explanation.

Where apparent divergence exists, it is attributable to differences in design domain and stage emphasis rather than genuine disagreement. Sources focused on architecture and spatial design emphasize optimization and parametric systems[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025; Mr. Udit Gupta et al., 2025; Xiang Liu, 2026], while those focused on graphic and visual communication foreground image-generative tools[Kristin A. Bartlett & J. Camba, 2024; Saja A. Hafez, 2025; Gerry Derksen & Sejal Bansal, 2024]. Sources addressing early ideation highlight AI's capacity to widen creative search spaces and reduce design fixation[Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025; Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026], while those addressing later stages emphasize simulation, evaluation, and documentation[Xiang Liu, 2026; Y. Lai et al., 2023]. These are complementary perspectives on the same underlying principle: AI's utility is stage-specific, and its value depends on the match between computational capability and the cognitive demands of a particular design task.

The tension between productivity gains and reflective depth—identified most explicitly by Stanimirović et al.[Mirko Stanimirović et al., 2026] and Willems et al.[Thijs Willems et al., 2025]—offers a mechanistic explanation for why studies sometimes report mixed educational outcomes. When AI is deployed instrumentally as a production accelerator, students generate more outputs but may engage in shallower conceptual reasoning[Mirko Stanimirović et al., 2026]. When AI is repositioned as a sparring partner or deliberately refused at certain stages, students develop stronger critical faculties and deeper design understanding[Mirko Stanimirović et al., 2026; Thijs Willems et al., 2025]. This pattern suggests that the mode of AI integration—not merely its presence or absence—determines its impact on design quality and learning.

For parametric customization and assistive product development specifically, the literature supports a layered integration strategy. Optimization and parametric systems (e.g., Finch 3D, Autodesk Generative Design) handle constraint-based exploration and variant generation[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025; Mr. Udit Gupta et al., 2025]. NLP interfaces lower barriers for non-specialist users to interact with complex parametric tools through semantic rather than technical commands[A. Troshkin & D. Makukhin, 2025; Samuelle Bourgault & J. E, 2023]. Generative systems accelerate visual prototyping and concept communication[Chang Xiong et al., 2026]. Decision-support analytics inform trade-off analysis across competing design criteria[Amin Mirzaei et al., 2026]. In each case, the designer retains the irreducible responsibilities of problem definition, contextual interpretation, cultural sensitivity, and ethical judgment[Xiang Liu, 2026; Mr. Udit Gupta et al., 2025; Wilmer Gonzalo Chaca Espinoza, 2025]—capacities that AI systems, as the corpus consistently demonstrates, do not possess.
