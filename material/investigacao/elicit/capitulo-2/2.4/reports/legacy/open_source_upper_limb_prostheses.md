# Open-Source 3D-Printable Upper-Limb Prostheses: e-NABLE, Victoria Hand Project, and the Evidence Base

# Open-Source 3D-Printable Upper-Limb Prostheses: e-NABLE, the Victoria Hand Project, and What the Evidence Shows

## Origins and organisational models

The e-NABLE community began in 2013, when a prop-maker in the US Pacific Northwest and a carpenter in South Africa collaborated to produce what is often described as the first home-fabricated, 3D-printed hand and released the design as open source; a Google-map "mashup" linking people who needed devices with people willing to print them followed shortly after, and by mid-2016 e-NABLE had grown to more than 8,600 online members who had delivered around 2,000 hands to children in 50 countries [^1]. Structurally, e-NABLE is a global, distributed, loosely coordinated volunteer network of 3D-printing enthusiasts who design, fabricate and ship upper-limb assistive devices — mostly to children, but also to some adults — largely outside the formal prosthetics service chain [^2][^3].

The Victoria Hand Project (VHP) operates on a very different model: a Canadian charity that partners with local prosthetic care providers in low- and middle-income countries to deliver 3D-printed upper-limb prostheses through a clinical pathway rather than a maker-to-recipient one [^4]. VHP's own retrospective explicitly frames its journey around affordability and customisation on the benefit side, and quality control plus the "steep learning curve" of the digital 3D workflow on the challenge side [^4].

Both projects sit inside a broader ecosystem — Enabling the Future, Thingiverse, the NIH 3D Print Exchange — that a 2025 review catalogued as hosting 35 distinct 3D-printed upper-limb prosthesis designs, of which 64% cost under USD 50 and most were rated easy-to-moderate to assemble; e-NABLE is called out as having played a "significant role" in the development and dissemination of these devices [^5].

## The e-NABLE device catalog

When people say "e-NABLE hand" they usually mean one of a handful of designs, but the community wiki actually maintains an active catalog organised by anatomy (hand vs. arm), actuation (wrist vs. elbow), and status (currently recommended vs. "other") [^6]. Most hand designs assume a functional wrist that can bend at least 30° in either direction plus most of the palm, since finger flexion is driven by wrist motion; users who lack that anatomy are routed instead to elbow-actuated arm designs [^7].

**Hand designs — currently recommended (wrist-actuated):** e-NABLE Phoenix Hand v3, Phoenix v2 Hand, Kinetic Hand, Osprey Hand, and Unlimbited Phoenix Hand [^6][^7].

**Hand designs — "other" (older or less-recommended, but still catalogued):** Aesthetic Passive Hand, MotoGripper Terminal Device, Cyborg Beast, Flexy-Hand 2, The Paraglider, Raptor Reloaded, K1 Hand, Talon, Ody Hand, and Forefinger Gripper Hand [^6].

**Arm designs — currently recommended (elbow-actuated):** Unlimbited Arm v2.1, Gripper-Kwawu Long Forearm, and Gripper-Kwawu Short Forearm [^7][^6].

**Arm designs — "other":** El Medallo Bionic Arm, Phoenix Reborn Arm, Adjustowrap Gripper Arm, Flexy Arm, Po Arm, Kwawu Arm, VEOG (Versatile Elbow Operated Gripper), plus a self-suspending below-elbow socket methodology [^6].

**Terminal devices, fingers and the NIOP modular kit:** Gripper Thumb (terminal device and bionic attachment), Lobster Thumb, Knick Finger, Flex Finger, Frog Arm exoskeleton and Bike Handle Adapter sit alongside the No-Insurance Optimized Prosthetics (NIOP) module family — Helix Cuff, NIOP Hook, Humeral/Radial Conductor, Kwawu Bionic, Kwawu Hand/Socket/Thermoform NIOP Remix, Moto Gripper with QC, Salvation guitar-pick adapters, Shelbow and Shelbow 2.0, XO-Shoulder / XO-Shoulder Robusto, and various brace and wrist-pin parts [^6].

Above-elbow amputees are explicitly flagged as an unsolved case inside e-NABLE: the wiki notes those designs are "a work-in-progress" and points users either to the NIOP modules or to external organisations — Limbitless Solutions, Open Bionics, the Victoria Hand Project, and Exiii — rather than a native e-NABLE arm [^7]. That gap is the main reason the Victoria Hand Project shows up as a sibling ecosystem rather than a competitor inside the e-NABLE literature.

The practical implication for anyone reading the evidence base is that the devices studied most heavily in academic papers — Cyborg Beast, Raptor / Raptor Reloaded, Phoenix / Phoenix Reborn — are drawn largely from the "other" (older) tier of the catalog [^6], while the currently recommended line has shifted toward Phoenix v3, Kinetic, Osprey and Unlimbited on the hand side and Unlimbited Arm v2.1 / Gripper-Kwawu on the arm side [^6]. Clinical validation and mechanical-testing evidence therefore lags the current recommended line by at least one design generation.

## Customisation and personalisation

The technical promise across the literature is remarkably consistent: 3D printing offers "higher availability and accessibility" and can produce "complex geometrical and highly customized products, which are essential features for prostheses manufacturing" [^8]. Concretely, the Cyborg Beast — arguably the most-studied e-NABLE lineage device — was designed as a low-cost, wrist-driven transitional hand for children, with an explicit distance-fitting procedure so families in developing countries or without access to clinicians can be measured and fitted remotely [^9]. A follow-up study on remote fitting reported acceptable QUEST satisfaction scores, with weight (4.50 ± 0.76), safety (4.38 ± 0.52) and ease of use (4.13 ± 0.64) rating highest, and argued that remote fitting has "significant potential for rapid fabrication of functional prostheses to developing countries due to increased availability of digital devices in rural areas" [^10].

Diment et al.'s systematic review is blunt on the point that the customisation story has been oversold to date: across the eight studies it identified, "the devices were designed to prioritise reduction of manufacturing costs, not customisability for comfort and function," and the potential of 3D printing for individual customisation "has yet to be fully realised."[^11] A 2025 review reaches a similar conclusion at the population level: "variability in design, a lack of standardized manufacturing protocols, and limited clinical validation remain challenges" [^5].

## Functional and clinical evidence

Clinical evaluations of open-source 3D-printed hands are almost entirely small-sample, uncontrolled, and short-follow-up. A prospective observational study of 14 children in India using an e-NABLE-style body-powered hand found that Box and Block gross-dexterity scores improved from a mean of 24 to 35 with the prosthesis (p = 0.049), but UBET and ABILHAND scores went in the wrong direction — attributed to the design's lack of lateral and tripod pinch — and the authors positioned the device as a *transitional* prosthesis rather than a definitive one [^12]. Zuniga et al.'s longitudinal work on the Cyborg Beast in 11 children (aged 3–15) similarly showed a significant hand × time interaction for gross manual dexterity but not for wrist strength after ~24 weeks of use [^13]. In Nepal, an e-NABLE deployment to 76 children found that participants could lift only light objects (tray, tennis ball, water bottle), struggled with complex movements, and only 61.8% were "completely satisfied", with tear of the rubber band or cord and broken parts cited as the leading reasons for dissatisfaction [^14].

The two most-cited systematic reviews take a hard evidentiary line. Diment et al. (2017) searched three databases, identified 321 records and included only eight studies — five case studies and three small case series — with no controls; all showed "promising results, but none demonstrated external validity, avoidance of bias or statistically significant improvements over conventional prostheses" [^11]. Atallah et al.'s 2025 PRISMA review of 1,420 records included only 11 studies and, while it reports "improved dexterity, range of motion (ROM), and user satisfaction," qualifies this with "small sample sizes, variability in assessment tools, and limited high-quality evidence" [^15]. Abbady et al.'s 2021 review specifically on LMICs included 18 studies and concluded that low material cost, aesthetics and personalised fitting make 3D-printed prostheses "a potential solution", but that "the lack of (homogeneous) data shows the need for more published (scientific) research" [^16].

## Safety and durability

Safety and durability are the recurrent weak points. Diment et al. warn that "until randomised controlled trials with follow-up are performed, the comfort, functionality, durability and long-term effects on quality of life remain unknown" [^11]. Cabibihan et al. mechanically evaluated the most popular open-source body-powered designs and found that the hand can perform only 3 of the 33 human grasp postures, and that cables and materials could withstand 22 N of normal grasping force — enough to meet accessibility-design standards but well below able-bodied hand capabilities — with total build cost as low as USD 19 [^17].

Romero et al. explicitly frame this gap as a safety concern: "3D-printed hand prostheses lacking mechanical feasibility studies... may exhibit various issues like durability shortcomings, anatomical incompatibility, functional safety concerns, manufacturing quality deficiencies, and health risks due to inappropriate materials," including "damage or premature failure during use, discomfort, skin irritation, injuries, inability to withstand functional loads, and health hazards from toxic or allergenic substances."[^18] Ramos et al. quantified grip force and traction for compliant open-source hand designs (PrHand1: 23.4 ± 1.5 N grip, 173 N traction; PrHand2: 36.1 ± 2.3 N grip) as a first step toward standardised mechanical benchmarking [^19], and Uscanga-Tlalpan and Diaz-Arango tested PLA finger and PET-G forearm structures with two joint schemes under repeated loading using GOM Correlate to characterise structural integrity [^20]. These are proof-of-method exercises, not standards-compliant qualification testing.

Regulatory framing is essentially absent from most e-NABLE-style deployments. Manero et al. explicitly situate their case-study review within the "medical regulatory framework in the United States" while noting that the grassroots maker community operates largely outside it [^21].

## Usability, acceptance and lived experience

The user-experience literature is small but pointed. Şahinol's field study of children with upper-limb differences using e-NABLE devices found that the device "is not only a socially enabling somatechnic but can itself be limiting, vulnerable and painful for children due to its materiality," and that the community's iconography of superhero/heroine identities is not always in the child's interests [^22]. The paper concludes, in line with crip technoscience, that "3D printed prostheses should be co-developed with (and specifically for) them" [^22].

A qualitative study of Indian mothers of children fitted with 3D-printed prostheses reported that acceptability was moderate within close family but that "social stigmatization was still a barrier to continued use", and mothers cited "functional limitations, pain, and unmet expectations" as reasons for abandonment [^23]. In Chile, Cyborg Beast users and their caregivers reported that "the prosthesis did not meet the expectations of use and esthetic" and identified materiality and design as the source of the difficulties [^24]. Hawthorn and Ashbrook's first-person account, in contrast, documents a positive trajectory from device recipient to active participant and advocate for self-efficacy within e-NABLE [^2]. These accounts sit alongside more traditional acceptance work showing that adopting a device early and matching it to the child's actual daily activities remain the biggest determinants of continued use.

Parry-Hill et al.'s interview study of 14 e-NABLE stakeholders — volunteers and clinicians — surfaced a structural tension that is specific to distributed DIY assistive-technology work: the two groups are complementary in skills and both motivated by helping others, but "their different perceptions of risk may result in uneven outcomes or missed expectations for end users" [^3].

## User involvement, co-design and design methodology

Where the literature is strongest and most directly useful for an industrial-design thesis is on co-design and user-centred design as antidotes to abandonment. Figliolia et al. document a user-centred design case study for a customised 3D-printed transradial prosthesis in which "the participation of the user in the design process using a user-centered design approach lead to a customized product that matched the user's preferences" and, they argue, thereby reduced abandonment risk [^25]. Ccorimanya et al. extended this into a three-year, iterative UCD case study for very young children with congenital below-elbow deficiency, with parents mediating between child and design team and multiple prototypes tuned to different growth stages [^26]. Chu et al.'s "3-corner" participatory-design protocol — occupational therapist, engineer, experienced child user and parent — explicitly targets the "prosthesis-related factors in functionality and appearance as well as the training and prosthetics services" that drive paediatric rejection [^27].

Jones et al.'s co-creation survey and workshop with users, clinicians, academics and industry ranked six themes; in order of user priority these were **function, psychology, aesthetics, clinical service, collaboration, and media** [^28]. Aesthetic co-design is treated as a substantive dimension in its own right: Ibanez-Arricivita worked with three Paralympian amputees to translate psychosocial meanings into visual and aesthetic language, framing prostheses as "symbolic objects for purposes of self-expression and advocacy" [^29].

## Relevance for industrial design and for a parametric/AI-driven generator

For a thesis on a web-based, parametric prosthetic-hand generator, the open-source 3D-printed hand literature offers several actionable points.

- **The customisation gap is a real design problem, not just a marketing one.** The current open-source designs are cheap and printable but their customisation is largely limited to scaling and colour choice; comfort- and function-critical geometry is not truly parametric to the individual [^11][^5]. A parametric-plus-AI generator sits precisely in this gap.
- **Remote fitting has been demonstrated but its measurement pipeline is fragile.** Zuniga et al. show that distance/remote fitting can work in principle and yields acceptable satisfaction scores, but the process still relies on hand measurements and clinician judgement [^10]. A well-designed web platform can standardise this input step.
- **Safety and durability need to be first-class design constraints, not afterthoughts.** Multiple mechanical-testing papers and Romero et al.'s FEA study argue that the field lacks standards-compliant mechanical qualification of open-source designs [^18][^19][^20]. A generator that produces geometry should also produce (or at least surface) predictions of stress, grip force, and expected failure modes.
- **Co-design is the strongest mitigator of abandonment.** UCD- and participatory-design-based projects consistently reduce rejection risk relative to purely engineering-led development [^25][^26][^27][^28]. A web platform is a natural vehicle for iterative user-in-the-loop customisation — but only if the interaction design lets users actually explore variants and their consequences.
- **The regulatory frame cannot be ignored.** Manero et al. and Diment et al. both warn that open-source devices sit uneasily inside medical-device regulation and that the evidentiary base for clinical claims is thin [^21][^11]. Any generator intended for clinical use will need a documented clinical-validation pathway, not just STL output.

## What is still missing

Across three systematic reviews and 15+ years of activity, the same shortlist recurs: (i) no randomised controlled trials, (ii) small heterogeneous samples with non-comparable outcome measures, (iii) minimal long-term durability and safety data, (iv) an under-explored gap between what a low-cost 3D-printed hand *can do mechanically* and what the user *actually needs and adopts*, and (v) a governance question about how volunteer-fabricated devices interact with formal prosthetic care [^11][^15][^16][^5][^3]. For thesis framing, this is the argumentative wedge: the technology and the community exist and have delivered thousands of devices, but the design methodology — customisation logic, evaluation instruments, safety qualification, and clinical integration — is still catching up.
