| Traditional Manual (Callipers / Tape / Goniometers) | Uses physical instruments like calipers, tape measures, rulers, and goniometers to directly measure linear distances, circumferences, and joint angles on the human body based on anatomical landmarks.[1, 2, 3, 4, 5] | Instrument accuracy can be high (e.g., digital calipers accurate to 0.01mm).[6] However, comparisons show differences of approximately 0.9 mm to 1.11 mm when compared with 3D scanning and MRI, respectively.[7, 1] One study found manual measurements to be more accurate than 2D/3D scanner methods for hand length and breadth.[8] Repeatability is a known issue.[1, 2] | Measurement takes approximately 20 minutes for a set of dimensions.[1] | Lowest cost method.[7, 1] | Discrete 1D linear and angular measurements.[3] | Low cost, practical, and does not require extensive technical expertise.[1] Well-established with standardized procedures (e.g., ISO 7250).[4, 9] | This method can suffer from poor repeatability and large variances, particularly when measurements are taken by different people.[1, 2] The subjective nature of aligning tools with landmarks can also cause variation.[3] | Studies using this method often employ tools such as a stadiometer, Martin-type sliding calipers, digital calipers (accurate to 0.01mm), goniometers, and tape measures.[6, 1, 10, 2, 11, 5, 4] |
| 3D Scanning (Structured Light / Laser) | A non-contact method that captures the 3D shape of an object. Structured light scanners project a light pattern (e.g., stripes) onto the surface and use a camera to infer the 3D geometry from the deformation of the pattern. Laser scanners use a laser line or point to measure the distance to the surface.[12, 13] Data is captured as a 3D point cloud, which is then processed into a mesh model.[14] | Resolution varies by device, from 0.05 mm to 4 mm.[7] Reported accuracy figures include: Artec Leo (1.16–1.46% relative error), Occipital Structure Sensor (5.08% error).[15] One study found 3D scan data to be accurate within 96% of caliper measurements.[12] Comparative studies show a difference of about 1.11 mm from MRI and 0.9 mm from traditional methods.[1, 7] Volumetric deviation is reported between 2-9%.[1] CT scanning has similar precision for collecting surface dimensions.[15] | Fast acquisition time, with scans taking from a few minutes (~4 minutes for 18 angles) to as little as a few seconds.[7, 1, 13] Total processing time to get a 3D model can be around 24 minutes.[7, 1] Some challenges, like keeping the hand still, can extend the process to up to two minutes.[16] | Considered relatively low-cost and cost-effective compared to MRI/CT.[7, 1, 12] Per-scan fees are reported to be between $10 and $20.[7, 1] Equipment costs vary widely, from ~$500 for consumer-grade sensors (Occipital Structure Sensor) to over $34,800 for high-end systems (Artec Leo).[15] A custom scanner can be built for ~$40 on top of the cost of a RealSense camera.[14] | A 3D point cloud which is processed into a triangular mesh (e.g., STL format).[7, 16, 14, 1, 13] | Provides a rapid, non-invasive, and highly detailed digital model of the limb, enabling the design of personalized and anatomically conformal sockets and devices.[7, 12, 13, 3] The output data can be directly integrated into CAD/CAM and 3D printing workflows.[17, 18] It has high repeatability and reliability.[7] | Can be less accurate than MRI for volumetric measurements and may have lower data precision than direct methods.[1, 2] Requires processing to convert point clouds to usable models, which can be complex.[15] The quality can be affected by movement artefacts if the subject is not still.[16] | Scanners: Artec Eva, Artec Leo, 3d3 Solutions HDI Advance R1, HP SLS-2, Intel RealSense, Occipital Structure Sensor.[1, 16, 14, 8, 15, 19] Software: Flexscan3D PRO, MeshLab, CloudCompare, Rapidform XOR, Geomagic Studio, SolidWorks.[7, 1, 16, 15, 14] |
| CT (Computed Tomography) | A medical imaging technique that uses X-rays and computer processing to create cross-sectional images (slices) of the body. It can generate detailed 3D models of both hard (bone) and soft tissues.[7, 1] CT images were used to create a hand skeleton model for prosthesis design.[20, 21] | Reported to provide 0.4 mm planar resolution and 0.5 mm z-axis resolution.[7, 1] A study comparing it with a source CT scan found a relative mean distance of 0.34 mm for a prosthetic arm and 0.46 mm for a socket.[22] It has similar precision to structured light scanning for surface dimensions.[15] | Not mentioned in source(s). | Considered to have high equipment and infrastructure costs, restricting use to specialized environments like hospitals.[15] | A series of 2D cross-sectional images (e.g., DICOM format), which can be reconstructed into a 3D volumetric model or surface mesh.[7, 1, 21] | Provides high-resolution 3D models that clearly distinguish between bone and soft tissue, allowing for highly accurate, patient-specific design of sockets and internal prosthetic structures.[7, 22, 1] It offers high accuracy, with a reported Dice Similarity Coefficient of 0.96 for a prosthetic arm and 0.95 for a socket compared to the source CT scan.[22] | Involves exposing the patient to ionizing radiation.[3] Equipment is expensive and not easily accessible outside of clinical settings.[15] | Software used for processing CT data for prosthetic design includes 3D Slicer, ScanIP, and SolidWorks.[21] One study used a series of 539 CT images.[21] |
| MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) | A medical imaging technique that uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves to generate detailed images of the body's internal structures. It is particularly effective for imaging soft tissues.[7, 1] | Can achieve a resolution of 0.5 mm in all three axes.[7, 1] It has a reported volumetric measurement deviation of around 2%, which is better than that of 3D scanners (2-9%).[1] A study found no statistically significant difference in measurements compared to traditional and 3D scanning methods (p<0.98).[7, 1] | The scan of a hand takes approximately 30 minutes.[7, 1] Including image processing, the total time to acquire a 3D model is around 70 minutes.[7, 1] | The most expensive method, with scan fees reported between $15 and $150.[7, 1] Additional costs are incurred for the 3D reconstruction software required.[1] | A series of 2D cross-sectional images (e.g., DICOM format) which can be reconstructed into a 3D volumetric model or surface mesh (STL).[7, 1] | Excellent for visualizing soft tissues and capturing internal anatomical details without using ionizing radiation.[7, 1] It demonstrated the lowest deviation among traditional, 3D scanning, and MRI methods in one comparative study.[1] | High cost and long acquisition times are significant drawbacks.[7, 1] The equipment is large, expensive, and not portable, limiting accessibility to clinical settings.[15] Patients must remain very still during the long scan.[7] | Siemens Symphony Vision MRI scanner; Mimics (Materialize) software for 3D reconstruction from MRI data.[1] |
| Radiography (X-ray) | Uses X-rays to create a 2D projection image of the body's internal structures, primarily bone. This method can be used to measure the lengths and widths of bones like the metacarpals and phalanges from the resulting images.[23, 10, 3] It can be used to extract dimensions and shape for a prosthesis from the healthy hand of a patient.[24] | Described as providing "accuracy in measurement of bony dimensions," but no specific numerical error values are reported.[10] One study reports measurements with a standard deviation, such as a proximal phalanx length of 4.56 ± 0.24 cm.[10] | Not mentioned in source(s). | Not mentioned in source(s). | 2D digital image (roentgenogram).[23] | Provides accurate measurements of internal bony dimensions and geometry, which is useful for designing components that interface with the skeleton or need to replicate skeletal kinematics.[10, 23] The dimensions extracted can be used to define the length of a prosthesis.[25] | Exposes the subject to ionizing radiation.[3] The 2D projection may not accurately measure rotations or complex 3D geometry.[3] May not be suitable for capturing soft tissue details. | Studies have used digital radiographs of hand and wrist joints (P/A view) and software such as MB Ruler to measure bone dimensions from the images.[10] |
| Photogrammetry | A non-contact technology that creates 3D models from a series of 2D photographs taken from different angles. By identifying common points across multiple images, the software can triangulate the 3D position of those points to reconstruct the object's shape.[2, 3] | One study compared 2D photo-based measurements (a form of photogrammetry) with a conversion factor of 0.08618 pixels/mm, but no formal accuracy validation against a ground truth was provided.[26] Other sources discuss evaluating its precision and accuracy but do not provide specific numbers in the available text.[8] | Not mentioned in source(s). | Not mentioned in source(s). | 3D point cloud or mesh model, similar to 3D scanning.[2, 13] | A portable and non-contact method that can be used for fieldwork and national surveys, making it flexible for data collection outside of a lab.[2, 13] It can be used to create 3D models for CAD-based design workflows. | Can be a complex process requiring multiple images and specialized software for processing. Not as accurate as direct measurements in some comparisons.[2, 8] | Commercial photogrammetry solutions mentioned in the literature include Sensory Markers and Photomodeler.[2] One study mentions using a Nikon DX AF-NIKKOR camera and ImageJ software in a process described as "photoanthropometric image analysis."[27, 26] |
| Computer Vision / Image Processing (OpenCV, Mediapipe, etc.) | This category involves using algorithms to extract measurements from 2D images or video streams. Techniques include edge detection, contour analysis, and machine learning models for landmark identification and hand tracking.[17] This can be done with a reference object for scale or by using sophisticated models that recognize hand parts directly.[17] | Accuracy is a key factor assessed but no specific numerical error values are provided in the source texts. The process involves comparing findings with theoretical values to gauge performance.[17] One study noted that 2D scanning is less accurate (8.7–16% relative error) than 3D scanning.[15] | Can operate in real-time for applications like landmark tracking.[17] However, no specific acquisition time for a full set of anthropometric measurements is provided. | Generally low-cost, as it can be implemented using standard consumer-grade cameras and open-source software libraries.[17] | Discrete dimensional measurements (lengths, angles), 2D or 3D coordinates of keypoints/landmarks.[17] | Enables remote and non-contact measurement, increasing accessibility for users.[17] The extracted dimensions can be used as direct input for parametric CAD software to create customized prosthetic designs.[17] It has the potential for real-time applications.[17] | Accuracy can be lower than direct measurement or 3D scanning and is sensitive to factors like lighting, camera angle, and background.[17] 2D image analysis cannot measure the curvature and full 3D silhouette of the hand.[15] Machine learning models require large datasets for training. | Open-source libraries are commonly used, including OpenCV for image processing, Mediapipe for hand tracking and landmark identification, and TensorFlow for machine learning analysis.[17] ImageJ is also used for analyzing 2D images.[28, 27, 26] |
